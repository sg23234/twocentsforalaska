Connectivity

Coverage: FCC data through Dec 2025 · prices: Mar 2026 · grants: Feb 2026 approval · draft
The read

Alaska is the hardest state in the country to connect, and the numbers show both the gap and the money aimed at it. At the end of 2025, a quarter of Alaska homes could not get wired or fixed-wireless internet at 100 megabits, and the average internet plan on offer cost $110 a month, the most of any state. Even so, 95% of households pay for some kind of internet, helped by fast-spreading satellite service: about one in ten Alaskans running speed tests is on Starlink. Federal programs have paid Alaska telecom companies $4.6 billion since 2016, and a new $629 million grant round will reach about 46,500 more homes and businesses, about half by fiber and a third by satellite. Beyond the internet, 86% of communities have no road connection, so subsidized flights and air-shipped bypass mail carry people and groceries.

How Alaska compares

Alaska combines the country's hardest geography with its largest per-person telecom subsidy. A quarter of homes still lacked 100/20 wired or fixed-wireless service at the end of 2025, and advertised plans averaged about $29 a month more than the national average. Satellite has changed the picture quickly: about one in ten Alaskans running speed tests used Starlink, compared with one in 67 Californians, and the new federal grants put a third of locations on satellite at a small fraction of the per-location cost of fiber.

Data notes

  • The 25% figure counts fiber, cable, copper and licensed fixed wireless at 100/20 Mbps, from FCC Broadband Data Collection filings as of December 2025, analyzed by Cartesian. It leaves out satellite. Cartesian's state chart lists Alaska first but its text does not state a national comparison on the same basis.
  • The household subscription rate is from the Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey, via America's Health Rankings, and counts any broadband subscription, including satellite and cellular data plans.
  • Prices are averages of advertised internet-only plans, not what households pay. The Starlink share comes from Ookla speed tests, which people choose to run, so it is a rough guide to use.
  • Cost per location divides each award's reported dollars by its reported locations; several are rounded or minimum figures. Fiber grants pay for new lines in the ground; satellite awards pay for service on a network that already exists.
  • The $4.6 billion is ProPublica's total of federal Universal Service subsidies to Alaska telecom companies since 2016.
  • To add before launch: FCC coverage by borough, cellular coverage, barge and air freight rates, and Essential Air Service fares and on-time records.
