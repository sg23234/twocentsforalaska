Port of Alaska tonnage

Draft · figures updated by hand

Most goods that reach Southcentral and Interior Alaska come through the Don Young Port of Alaska in Anchorage. Tonnage crossing its docks is a direct measure of how much the state is consuming and building.

The longer view

The Army Corps of Engineers has counted waterborne freight at Anchorage for decades. Its count covers cargo moved by vessel in the harbor; the port's own dock count also includes fuel piped through its valve yard, so the two series aren't directly comparable.

Rebuilding the port

The Port of Alaska Modernization Program is a $2.76 billion plan to replace the aging docks, paid for largely by a per-ton surcharge on cargo.

Milestones

  • 2003–2013: a federally managed expansion raises about $439 million, then is abandoned when its dock design proves unsuitable.
  • 2014: the city starts the current, scaled-back modernization program.
  • 2022: the new petroleum and cement terminal opens.
  • March 2026: work starts on Cargo Terminal 1 ($977 million, due 2029).
  • July 2026: the federal government settles the expansion lawsuit for $191.3 million, which goes to construction.

What shippers pay

  • The modernization surcharge is $8.05 per ton of cargo in 2026.
  • It rises to $22.76 per ton in January 2027.

Data notes

  • Dock tonnage is in short tons (2,000 lb) as the port reports it. It counts weight, not value or container counts.
  • "Dockside" fuel arrives by tanker or barge; "shoreside" fuel moves through the port's valve yard by pipeline. The split shifts sharply around 2015 after the North Pole refinery closed, so compare total fuel over long periods.
  • The port revises recent years between reports; this page uses the latest figure for each year.
  • Army Corps figures for 2002 onward are published in an online database that isn't yet loaded here.
