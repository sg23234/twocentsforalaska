Current account

Draft · figures updated by hand

Alaska is a small open economy: most of what it consumes is shipped in and most of what it produces is sold outside the state. A current account adds up what Alaskans earn from the rest of the world and what they pay out, using the framework countries use for their balance of payments. No official statistic does this for a state, so the balance here is an estimate for 2024, built line by line, with a range and a confidence grade for each line.

The short version

In 2024, Alaskans earned more from the rest of the world than they paid out: a surplus of roughly $6.5B, about 9% of the state's GDP. The likely range runs from $3B to $10B, because several lines have to be estimated.

The surplus does not come from trade. Alaska sells about $36B of goods and services to the outside each year and buys about $34B, so trade roughly balances (+$2.1B). Income going out to nonresident workers, out-of-state owners and lenders ($10.9B) also roughly cancels investment income coming in ($10.4B).

What tips the balance is the federal government. Counting its payroll, purchases, grants and benefits against the federal taxes Alaskans pay, about $12.8B more arrives from Washington than leaves for it. Without that, Alaska would run a deficit of about $6.3B.

The account at a glance

A current account adds up every payment between a place and the rest of the world that is not a loan, an investment or a sale of assets. Countries publish one; states do not, so this is an estimate.

Money coming in

Every payment from outside Alaska to Alaskans, largest first. Federal flows total $19.6B, more than crude oil ($13.6B).

Money going out

Every payment from Alaskans to the outside. Imports are by far the largest, at about $34B.

The federal government's part

Federal money arrives four ways and leaves one way. On balance about $12.8B a year comes in, roughly 18% of GDP.

Why federal payroll and purchases count as exports

A national current account treats a foreign embassy's spending like any other export: the customer sits outside the country. The same logic applies here. When the Defense Department pays a soldier at JBER or a contractor building a hangar at Eielson, the customer is in Washington, so the payment is a sale of services to the outside.

Grants and benefits are different. The federal government gets nothing in return, so they are counted as transfers, like foreign aid or remittances. Federal income and payroll taxes paid by Alaskans are the matching transfers going out.

For comparison, a 2026 ISER presentation put federal spending in Alaska at just under $20 billion in 2023, or about $18 billion without temporary pandemic money. This page's gross figure for 2024, $19.6B, is in that range. An earlier ISER estimate of $13 billion to $14 billion a year counted fewer streams.

Oil's part

Crude oil is the largest single export, but much of its value leaves again as profit for out-of-state owners.

How oil prices move the balance

North Slope fields produce about 170 million barrels a year, so every $10 change in the price changes the value of oil exports by about $1.7B.

Most of that change ends up with the producers, whose owners are outside Alaska, so it leaves again as profit. What stays is mainly the state's share. At recent prices, royalties and production tax take roughly 40% of an extra dollar, so a $10 rise in the price improves Alaska's current account by only about $0.7B. That share is an approximation; the production tax's sliding scale makes it shift with price.

This is why oil price swings shake the state budget more than they shake the current account.

Investment income: the Permanent Fund and household savings

Alaskans own large savings invested outside the state, publicly through the Permanent Fund and privately through retirement accounts and pensions.

Which Permanent Fund number belongs in the account

National accounts count interest, dividends and rent as income, and leave out capital gains, which are treated as a change in the value of assets. Following that rule, this page counts $1.83B of Permanent Fund income for 2024.

The Legislature budgets from a broader measure. Adding the Fund's realized gains, about $3.2B for 2024, would raise the current account to about +$9.7B.

The dividend is not in the account at all. The 2024 dividend and energy relief payment, about $1.06B, moved money from one Alaska account to Alaskans, so it is neither income from outside nor a payment to it.

Household investment income is the larger and less certain piece. BEA counts $10.8B of dividends, interest and rent received by Alaskans, including returns credited through pension funds such as the state retirement systems. This page assumes about 70% comes from outside Alaska. Each 10 percentage points on that assumption moves the balance by about $1.1B.

How the estimate is built

The goods-and-services balance

No agency counts what Alaska buys from the Lower 48, because there are no customs records between states. Instead, the balance is worked out from the national accounts identity: what a place produces, minus what it spends, equals what it sells to the outside on balance.

Alaska's GDP in 2024 was $71.6B. Alaskans spent $49.1B on household consumption (BEA), about $9.4B on private investment and about $11.0B through state and local government, for a total of $69.5B. The difference, +$2.1B, is the goods-and-services balance. Federal spending in Alaska is left out of the spending total because it is counted as an export.

The export lines are measured directly where possible. Imports are then whatever is needed to produce the balance. The split between goods and services is not reliable, so the page shows them together.

Three choices that had to be made

Federal payroll and purchases count as exports of services, for the reasons in the federal section.

Seafood is valued at first wholesale, the price processors get when the fish leaves their hands. Ex-vessel value, what fishermen are paid, would understate what Alaska sells.

Out-of-state owners' profits are counted after state taxes and royalties but before federal income tax. All of that profit leaves Alaska, whoever ends up with it.

What is not in the estimate

Capital gains and losses on investments, realized or not.

Pension payments to retirees who have left Alaska, and to those who have moved in. BEA's investment income figure partly captures these.

Loans, new investment and asset sales. Those belong to the financial account, which shows how a surplus or deficit is financed. Oil companies' spending on Willow and Pikka, for example, is an investment flowing in, not part of the current account; only its future profits will be.

Data notes

  • All figures are for calendar 2024 unless marked. Where a source uses the state fiscal year (July to June) or the federal fiscal year (October to September), the closest year or an average of two years is used, as described in each line.
  • The likely range combines each line's low-to-high range as if the errors were independent, which is a standard way to add up uncertain pieces. It is a guide, not a statistical confidence interval.
  • Seafood value includes fish processed at sea by Seattle-based catcher-processors, which BEA does not count in Alaska's GDP. Because imports are derived, this raises both exports and imports by the same amount and does not change the balance.
  • Crude oil exports count all North Slope production, including oil refined in Alaska. Refined products used in Alaska therefore show up in derived imports, and the balance is unaffected.
  • Spending in Alaska by nonresident workers is an export, like visitor spending, but is not measured separately. It is captured in the goods-and-services balance through the GDP identity.
  • No official statistic measures a state's current account. These are this site's estimates and will be revised as better data are found.
