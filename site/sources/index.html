About this site

Who makes it

Two Cents for Alaska is a personal project by Steven Gagliardo. Steven has spent his career in public-sector institutional investment management and is currently a portfolio manager on the private equity team at the Alaska Permanent Fund Corporation. He lives in Palmer.

Why it exists

The site began as a project to put AI tools to work on public data: gathering Alaska figures that are scattered across dozens of agencies, organizing them around how Alaska's economy actually works rather than how national dashboards are laid out, and building measures that only make sense here, such as electricity prices by region and the cost of Power Cost Equalization. The aim is to be informative and useful.

Disclaimer

This is a personal project. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Steven's employer or any government agency, and the views expressed here are his own. Every figure comes from the public sources listed on this page.

How AI is used

AI tools help gather data, write the code and draft the text. Figures are pulled from the sources below, and each page links to them so readers can check the numbers.

Micro · Input costs

Electricity

Shows
Average retail price of electricity in Alaska, all customer sectors combined, in cents per kilowatt-hour, and the percent change from the same month a year earlier.
Source
U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), Electric Power Monthly, retail sales of electricity by state and sector. Retrieved through the EIA Open Data API (electricity/retail-sales).
Series
State: Alaska (AK). Sector: all sectors (ALL). Measure: average price.
Method
The price is revenue divided by kilowatt-hours sold, as reported by utilities to EIA. The change compares the latest month with the same month one year earlier, which avoids seasonal swings.
Updates
Monthly, about two months after the month it covers. This site checks for new data on the 2nd and 16th of each month.
Notes
A statewide average. It blends lower Railbelt and Southeast hydro rates with much higher rates in rural communities, and EIA coverage of the smallest rural utilities is limited. Prices are before Power Cost Equalization credits.

Electricity page: utilities and regions

Shows
Average price per kWh for each Alaska utility and for groups of utilities by region, how each compares with the U.S. average, the change from the year before, and the monthly bill for 750 kWh.
Source
U.S. Energy Information Administration, Form EIA-861 Annual Electric Power Industry Report: Sales to Ultimate Customers (long form) and the Short Form (EIA-861S) for small utilities. Hawaii and Washington averages come from the same files.
Method
Price = revenue ÷ kWh sold, by customer type and for all customers. Region averages are weighted by kWh sold. Regions are Two Cents for Alaska's own grouping, loosely following Alaska Energy Authority energy regions, and are listed in data/manual/electricity-regions.json. Regions are ordered by customers served, and so are the utilities within each region. In the table, utilities with fewer than 1,000 customers are grouped into one row per region, priced by kWh-weighted average; they are still counted in region averages. The bill column is 750 kWh, about what a typical Alaska home uses in a month, times the utility's all-customer average price.
U.S. average
For all customer types, one figure is used for each year across the page: the U.S. all-sector average price from EIA's monthly data, averaged over the year weighted by kWh sold (revenue ÷ kWh, EIA's own method). U.S. averages computed from EIA-861 count only the energy-supplier row for customers in retail-choice states. The delivery utility's row repeats the same kWh and is counted for revenue only, so electricity is not counted twice.
Excluded
Napakiak Ircinraq Power Co. Its 2025 EIA-861 figures (214¢ per kWh, up 143% from 2024) look like a reporting error. Exclusions are listed in data/manual/electricity-regions.json.
Updates
Annual. EIA publishes final data for a year in the fall of the following year; this site picks up a new year automatically.
Notes
Covers utilities that report to EIA. Some of the smallest village utilities report only to the state's Power Cost Equalization program and are not included. Prices are before Power Cost Equalization credits.

Electricity page: Power Cost Equalization

Shows
How the state's Power Cost Equalization (PCE) credit is set, program totals and cost per Alaskan reached, a bar showing who pays for one kWh in villages served by AVEC, and an optional PCE estimate in the everyday-costs cards.
Source
Alaska Energy Authority, Power Cost Equalization program materials (program rules; FY2025: $46 million, 188 communities, 81,000+ Alaskans). Base rate from the Regulatory Commission of Alaska: 22.11¢ per kWh for FY2027 (bills from July 1, 2026), up from 20.38¢ for FY2026.
Per person
About $570 a year = $46 million paid in FY2025 ÷ 81,000 Alaskans who benefit, both from AEA. Includes the credit on community buildings.
Method
Credit per kWh = 95% × (cost − base rate), with cost capped at $1.00, on a home's first 750 kWh a month. The estimate uses each utility's average residential price from EIA-861 as its cost, or the all-customer price where no residential figure is reported.
Updates
By hand when the base rate changes (each July) or new program totals are published.
Notes
Estimates only. Actual PCE levels are set for each community from the utility's own costs and can be lower, and payments are cut in proportion when fund earnings fall short.

Electricity page: what changed recently

Shows
The five latest months of Alaska's average electricity price (all customers and households) and the change from a year earlier; and the three latest power plant changes, including capacity added and capacity lost.
Sources
Prices and sales: U.S. Energy Information Administration, Electric Power Monthly retail sales data, through the EIA API. Power plants: Form EIA-860M (monthly generator inventory), through the EIA API.
Method
Additions are the generators with the most recent first month of operation, labeled as a new plant (the whole plant started that month), a backup unit (EIA status standby/backup), a replacement (another unit at the plant retired the same month) or added capacity (the plant's total went up); otherwise "new unit." "Capacity down" covers retired units and plants whose total capacity fell from one month to the next. Labels that depend on month-to-month changes are found by comparing each monthly inventory with the one before, starting with November 2026 data. Generators of any size at plants of 1 MW or more are included.
Updates
Monthly, with the site's data refresh. EIA's figures run about two months behind.

Electricity page: what everyday things cost

Shows
The cost of common household uses at a chosen utility's price, next to the same use at the U.S. average household price.
Prices
Each utility's 2025 average residential price from EIA-861, or its all-customer price where no residential figure is reported. U.S.: EIA-861 residential average for 2025.
Energy use
Typical figures, set by Two Cents for Alaska from common appliance wattages: 10-minute hot shower with an electric water heater 3.5 kWh; full-size refrigerator for a month 40 kWh; 400 W block heater 4 hours a night for a month 48 kWh; electric dryer load 3 kWh; dishwasher load 1.2 kWh; electric oven for an hour 2.3 kWh; 1,500 W space heater 8 hours a day for a month 360 kWh; 100 W TV 4 hours a day for a month 12 kWh; electric car 100 miles 30 kWh; phone charging for a year 5 kWh. Actual use varies a lot by appliance and home.
PCE option
For eligible village utilities, the checkbox applies the estimated PCE credit described in the PCE entry. In the monthly figure it covers only the first 750 kWh.

Electricity page: household comparison

Shows
Monthly bill for a household using 750 kWh, at the average residential price per kWh, for five Alaska places (rural villages served by AVEC, Fairbanks, Mat-Su, Anchorage and Juneau, each by its utility), Hawaii, Washington, the U.S., Iceland, Norway and British Columbia.
Sources
Alaska, Hawaii, Washington and U.S.: EIA-861 residential averages. Iceland and Norway: Eurostat household electricity prices (nrg_pc_204), band 2,500–5,000 kWh a year, all taxes and levies, including state subsidies (Norway's electricity support); 2025 average of the two half-years. Iceland 20.2 and 20.8 euro cents per kWh; Norway 19.9 and 19.2. British Columbia: BC Utilities Commission, BC Hydro residential rate from April 2025 (basic charge, step 1 and step 2), at 750 kWh a month, with 5% GST. Exchange rates: Federal Reserve G.5A 2025 averages (CAD 1.3973 per USD; USD 1.1306 per EUR).
Updates
U.S. figures update automatically each year. The figures for Iceland, Norway and British Columbia are entered by hand in the page and need a yearly update.
Notes
U.S. figures exclude sales tax; the figures outside the U.S. include taxes. Alaska figures are before PCE credits.

Railbelt natural gas

Shows
Enstar's cost of gas each year; natural gas prices for Alaska and the U.S. by type of customer; Cook Inlet production by month and by field; gas in CINGSA storage; how cold the heating season has been in Anchorage; who uses the gas; and projects that could add supply.
Source
Prices: U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), Natural Gas Monthly, through the EIA Open Data API (natural-gas/pri/sum). Production and storage: Alaska Oil and Gas Conservation Commission (AOGCC), Data Miner full-table downloads (production since 2000 and storage, by well and month). Weather: NOAA Regional Climate Centers, Applied Climate Information System (ACIS), station ANC (Anchorage airport), with 1991–2020 normals. Enstar's cost of gas: its annual Gas Cost Adjustment filings with the Regulatory Commission of Alaska. Use by sector and projects: Alaska Department of Natural Resources, ISER, company announcements and news reports, listed on the page.
Series
EIA monthly series N3050 (city gate), N3010 (residential), N3020 (commercial) and N3045 (electric power), for Alaska and the U.S. AOGCC wells in the Cook Inlet Basin area. ACIS daily heating degree days (base 65°F).
Method
EIA prices are 12-month averages; an average needs at least 10 of the 12 months, since EIA withholds some Alaska months. Production adds up gas produced by every Cook Inlet well each month (gas and oil wells), divided by the days in the month; a latest month that looks only partly reported is left off. Field shares cover the latest 12 months. Storage is CINGSA's gas injected minus gas withdrawn, added up since it opened in 2012; this leaves out roughly 3.5 Bcf of gas already in the reservoir, so the level runs below CINGSA's reported inventory while changes match. Degree days are added up from July 1.
Updates
Prices, production, storage and degree days update automatically. EIA prices arrive two to three months after the month they cover, AOGCC production and storage about two months after; degree days through the day before. Production, storage and weather refresh every Monday as well as on the 2nd and 16th. Enstar's cost of gas changes each July. Hand-kept figures live in data/manual/natural-gas.json and show the date they were last checked.
