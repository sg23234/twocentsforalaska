Two Cents for Alaska/Dashboard 09

State capacity

Food aid: FY2025 · staffing: Nov 2025 · assets: FY2025 financial report and FY2027 budget · ferry: 2024 · draft
The read

Alaska's state government is delivering basic services with fewer hands than it is budgeted for. About one in six budgeted state jobs was vacant in late 2025. Food assistance shows the strain most clearly: the state processed only about two-thirds of new applications on time in federal fiscal 2025 and a quarter of renewals, and its payment error rate was the highest in the country for a fourth straight year. The state ferry system carries less than half the passengers it did in the early 1990s, with fewer ships in service. On buildings and roads, the state is holding its maintenance backlog steady rather than shrinking it: the FY2027 capital budget puts about $180 million toward deferred maintenance, roughly what the state estimated it needs each year just to keep a backlog of about $2.3 billion from growing.

How Alaska compares

Alaska's vacancy rate in state jobs has stayed near one in six since at least 2022, when it was close to one in five. On food assistance, Alaska ranked 50th of 50 states for timely renewals and second-lowest for new applications in fiscal 2025, and its error rate was about double the national rate. Under the 2025 federal budget law, states with high error rates will eventually pay part of benefit costs; Alaska is temporarily exempt, but a similar rule applied today would shift nearly $40 million a year to the state.

Capital spending looks large, but about 72% of the FY2027 capital budget is federal money. Against the $638 million the state's buildings, roads and equipment depreciated in fiscal 2025, deferred maintenance funding of about $180 million replaces a little over a quarter, enough to keep the backlog from growing but not to reduce it.

Over the past decade the state's governmental capital assets grew about $1.9 billion net of depreciation, but nearly all of that was roads, land and work in progress. Buildings ended fiscal 2025 at about the same book value as in 2015 and $327 million below their 2018 peak, a running deficit consistent with the state's large maintenance backlog.

Data notes

  • State capacity has no single official measure. This page tracks a few public services with regular, comparable data: staffing, food assistance processing, the ferry system and upkeep of state-owned buildings and roads.
  • Vacancy figures come from state budget data reported by the Anchorage Daily News. They count unfilled budgeted positions, not job postings.
  • USDA timeliness counts applicant-caused delays as untimely, so it is not a strict compliance measure.
  • Depreciation ($638M) and capital asset values ($9.72B net, up $355M) are for governmental activities in the FY2025 Annual Comprehensive Financial Report. They exclude the University of Alaska, which reports separately, so the backlog and depreciation figures cover somewhat different assets.
  • The running total uses net capital assets reported in each year's financial report, FY2015–FY2025; each report's prior-year figures match the previous report, so the series has no restatement breaks. Intangible assets, first reported separately in FY2022, are excluded. Values are historical cost, so a flat line means the real stock of buildings is shrinking as construction costs rise.
  • Alaska depreciates its roads and other infrastructure rather than using GASB's modified approach, which would replace depreciation with condition assessments (Alaska Administrative Manual 55).
  • Capital additions are estimated from the change in net assets because the financial report's capital-asset note, which lists additions and accumulated depreciation directly, was not available for this draft. That note would also give an asset-age measure: accumulated depreciation divided by original cost.
  • The governor's June 2026 line-item vetoes, about $89.5 million in total, left school maintenance and construction funding in place.
  • To add before launch: asset age and reinvestment from the ACFR capital-asset note, agency-by-agency backlog, permit processing times, DMV wait times, ferry on-time and cancellation records, and Division of Motor Vehicles and licensing backlogs.
