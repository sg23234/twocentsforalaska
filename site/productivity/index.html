Productivity

Annual data through 2025 · BLS releases each year in late May or June · draft
The read

Output per hour worked in Alaska's private economy fell 2.3% in 2025, the second-largest drop of any state, because hours worked rose 6.2% while output rose only 3.8%. Over the six years since 2019, Alaska's productivity grew 2.2% a year, slightly ahead of the U.S. at 2.0%. Over the longer run since 2007 it grew 0.8% a year, about half the national pace, and it swings far more from year to year than the U.S. figure does.

Output per hour since 2007

Alaska is 16% more productive per hour than in 2007. At its average pace, the U.S. would be about 33% higher.

Year-to-year change

Alaska's measure has moved more than 5% in either direction in nine of the last eighteen years.

What drove the change

Productivity is output growth minus hours growth, roughly. In 2025 hours outran output.

How Alaska compares

Alaska ranked first among states for productivity growth in some years and last in others. The level of output per hour in Alaska is high because oil and gas produce a great deal of value with few workers, and that same industry makes the year-to-year figure jumpy: when oil output or prices move, measured output can change sharply while hours barely move. Longer periods give a steadier read than any single year.

Data notes

  • Labor productivity is real output per hour worked in the private nonfarm sector. It excludes government and farms, so it covers most but not all of the economy.
  • BLS revises the whole history each year. Figures here are from the June 2026 release; earlier published numbers, such as the 2023 growth rate, will differ.
  • The U.S. line in the first chart is a reference path at the average rate, because the national index in this program was not available to us as a clean annual series. A live version would chart the published U.S. index.
  • To add before launch: productivity excluding oil and gas (real GDP per hour or per job outside mining), output per job by industry, and a peer comparison with other energy states such as North Dakota and Wyoming.
